# Product Context — Sanskrit Word Explorer

Grounded in CLAUDE.md, the canonical data files, and the shipped explorer.
Not invented for a design brief.

## Register

**product** — the explorer is a reference tool. The design serves the reading of
Sanskrit terms and their source passages; it is not itself the thing being sold.

## Product purpose

A browsable reference for 120 Sanskrit terms from the Yoga Sutras and the
principal Upanisads. Each term shows its morphological breakdown, its literal and
philosophical meaning, and, where it has been checked, the passage it comes from
in the original, quoted with a citation trail.

The project's governing commitment is source discipline: a term is labelled
"source-verified" only if a quoted passage and citation trail exist for it.
Currently 55 of 120 qualify. The interface must make that distinction legible
without making the unverified 65 look broken or shameful. They are working
entries, not errors.

## Users

One primary reader: a philosophy professor who teaches Indian philosophy with a
meditation emphasis (samadhi meditation). Very little technical background.
Secondary: students in that class following a link to a single term.

What they do with it:

- Look up one term they half-remember, often by meaning rather than by name
  ("absorption", "witness", "fear of death") rather than by spelling.
- Read the source passage to check that a claim is actually in the text.
- Link a single term into a syllabus, a slide, or an email.
- Browse a theme (the five kleshas, the four states) to prepare a lecture.

They are not looking for a dictionary to read front to back. They arrive with a
question about one word, or one group of words.

## Tone

Scholarly and quiet. The reader is an expert in the subject and a novice at
software; the interface should feel like a well-set reference book rather than an
app. Confidence without decoration. No enthusiasm, no onboarding chatter, no
celebration of the reader's actions.

The Sanskrit is the content. Devanagari and IAST must be set with real care:
correct diacritics, generous size, never cramped or clipped.

## Anti-references

- Dashboard and SaaS chrome. No metric tiles, no progress rings, no badges that
  gamify coverage.
- Language-learning apps (Duolingo and kin). No streaks, no encouragement, no
  playful mascots. This is scholarship, not practice.
- Wikipedia's dense grey slab of text. The reader is scanning for one thing.
- A "search-first, empty screen" interface. Browsing the corpus by theme is a
  real task, not a fallback for failed search.

## Strategic principles

1. **The quoted passage is the payload.** Everything else on a term card is
   apparatus. When a term is verified, its source text should be the thing the eye
   lands on after the term itself.
2. **Honest labelling over flattering labelling.** Verified and unverified are
   shown as a plain difference in kind, not as a score. Never imply the
   unverified entries are defective.
3. **One term is the unit.** Every term is addressable by URL, and a single term
   view should read well in isolation, printed or projected.
4. **120 items is a corpus, not a wall.** Browsing must be structured by source
   and theme so the reader never faces an undifferentiated field of chips.
5. **Offline and static, permanently.** No build step at read time, no fonts or
   scripts from third parties, no network dependency. It must work from a file
   on a laptop in a classroom with no wifi.

## Constraints

- Static HTML, CSS, and vanilla JS. No framework, no bundler.
- Deployed to GitHub Pages from `demo/`; `terms_inline.js` is generated by
  `sanskrit-etymology build-demo` and must not be hand-edited.
- Must render Devanagari correctly from system fonts alone.
